You don't write a single formula. You drag column names into place, and Excel does the adding up.</p><p class="prose__intro">This lesson builds one step by step with a made-up sales list, then shows two ways AI can help: Copilot inside Excel, or ChatGPT and Claude when you share only your column names. Menu names were checked in October 2026 in Excel for Microsoft 365 on Windows. On the web and on Mac a few buttons sit in different places, so look for the same words.</p></div><section id="prepare-the-data" class="prose lesson-section" aria-labelledby="prepare-the-data-h"><h2 id="prepare-the-data-h" class="lesson-h2">Get the list ready first</h2><p>Most PivotTable problems start in the list, not in the PivotTable. Our example is a sales list for three made-up shops. Each row is one sale: date, store, product, category and amount. For example: 3 March 2026, Palm Corner, Notebook, Stationery, SAR 1,200.</p><ul class="prose-list"><li>One header row at the top, with a short, unique name for every column. No empty headers, no two-row headers, no merged cells.</li><li>No blank rows or blank columns in the middle of the data.</li><li>One kind of data per column: dates in the date column, numbers in the amount column, and no text such as "n/a" mixed in.</li><li>Each column holds one thing. One "Date" column works better than separate columns for January, February and March.</li></ul><p>Then turn the list into an Excel Table:</p><ol class="steps"><li><span class="steps__n" aria-hidden="true">1</span><span>Click any cell in your list.</span></li><li><span class="steps__n" aria-hidden="true">2</span><span>Select Insert → Table (or Home → Format as Table, and pick a style).</span></li><li><span class="steps__n" aria-hidden="true">3</span><span>Check that "My table has headers" is ticked, then select OK.</span></li></ol><aside class="callout callout--tip"><p class="callout__title">Why a Table helps</p><p>When you add new rows to a Table, the PivotTable picks them up the next time you refresh. With a plain range, you have to change the source data by hand. If your list needs cleaning first, Lesson 3 shows how.</p></aside></section><section id="build-it" class="prose lesson-section" aria-labelledby="build-it-h"><h2 id="build-it-h" class="lesson-h2">Build the PivotTable</h2><ol class="steps"><li><span class="steps__n" aria-hidden="true">1</span><span>Click any cell in your Table.</span></li><li><span class="steps__n" aria-hidden="true">2</span><span>Select Insert → PivotTable.</span></li><li><span class="steps__n" aria-hidden="true">3</span><span>Choose New Worksheet, then select OK. Excel adds a blank PivotTable and opens the PivotTable Fields pane.</span></li><li><span class="steps__n" aria-hidden="true">4</span><span>Tick a field to add it, or drag it into one of the four areas at the bottom of the pane: Filters, Columns, Rows and Values.</span></li></ol><p>When you tick a field, Excel puts it in a default area: text usually goes to Rows and numbers to Values. Drag it somewhere else whenever you like. Nothing in your list changes, because the PivotTable works on its own copy. If the Fields pane disappears, click inside the PivotTable. Still hidden? Select PivotTable Analyze → Field List.</p><div class="table-wrap" role="region" aria-label="Which field goes where (sales by store and category)" tabindex="0"><table role="table"><caption>Which field goes where (sales by store and category)</caption><thead role="rowgroup"><tr role="row"><th scope="col" role="columnheader">Area</th><th scope="col" role="columnheader">What it does</th><th scope="col" role="columnheader">In our example</th></tr></thead><tbody role="rowgroup"><tr role="row"><th scope="row" role="rowheader">Rows</th><td role="cell" dir="auto">One line for each item, down the side</td><td role="cell" dir="auto">Store</td></tr><tr role="row"><th scope="row" role="rowheader">Columns</th><td role="cell" dir="auto">One column for each item, across the top</td><td role="cell" dir="auto">Category</td></tr><tr role="row"><th scope="row" role="rowheader">Values</th><td role="cell" dir="auto">The numbers to add up or count</td><td role="cell" dir="auto">Amount (Sum)</td></tr><tr role="row"><th scope="row" role="rowheader">Filters</th><td role="cell" dir="auto">One filter above the PivotTable for the whole report</td><td role="cell" dir="auto">Product</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>The result: one row per store, one column per category, a total in each cell, and totals at the end.</p><aside class="callout callout--tip"><p class="callout__title">Not sure where to start?</p><p>Select Insert → Recommended PivotTables. Excel looks at your data and offers a few ready layouts. Pick the closest one, then drag fields to adjust it.</p></aside></section><section id="shape-it" class="prose lesson-section" aria-labelledby="shape-it-h"><h2 id="shape-it-h" class="lesson-h2">Count, months, slicers and refresh</h2><p>Four small changes turn a basic PivotTable into a report you can reuse every month.</p><ul class="prose-list"><li>Sum or Count: numbers in Values are added up (Sum) by default. To count sales instead, select the arrow next to the field in the Values area → Value Field Settings → under Summarize Values By, choose Count → OK.</li><li>Group dates by month: put Date in Rows, right-click any date in the PivotTable → Group. Under By, choose Months (add Years if your list covers more than one year) → OK. Excel may group dates for you when you add them.</li><li>Filter with a slicer: click inside the PivotTable → Insert → Slicer (on the web: PivotTable Analyze → Slicer), tick Store → OK. Click a button to show one store, hold Ctrl to pick more, and Clear Filter shows them all again.</li><li>Refresh after new data: a PivotTable doesn't update by itself. Right-click anywhere in it → Refresh. With several PivotTables, use PivotTable Analyze → the arrow under Refresh → Refresh All.</li></ul><aside class="callout callout--warn"><p class="callout__title">"Count of Amount" is a warning sign</p><p>If you drag Amount into Values and Excel shows Count instead of Sum, it is reading some amounts as text. Fix that column first (Lesson 3 shows how), refresh, and compare the grand total with a quick SUM of the amount column in your list.</p></aside></section><section id="copilot-in-excel" class="prose lesson-section" aria-labelledby="copilot-in-excel-h"><h2 id="copilot-in-excel-h" class="lesson-h2">Let Copilot in Excel build it</h2><p>If your Microsoft 365 plan includes Copilot, you can describe the summary you want and let it build the PivotTable.
