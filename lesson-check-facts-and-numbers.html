When AI is wrong: check facts and numbers

AI can sound sure and still be wrong. Five quick checks to run before you use an answer.

  • Level: Beginner
  • 6 min read
  • Updated: 28 September 2026

On this page

  1. Why confident doesn't mean correct
  2. Five quick checks
  3. Numbers need extra care
  4. Sources that don't exist
  5. Common mistakes
  6. Try it now: 5 minutes

In this lesson you'll learn

  • Why AI sounds confident even when it is wrong
  • Five quick checks before you use an answer
  • Prompts that make mistakes easier to spot

AI tools write fluent, confident text whether it is correct or not. They can invent a statistic, a quote, a rule or a source that sounds completely real. This is often called "hallucination". Newer tools make fewer of these mistakes, but none make zero.

You don't need to distrust everything. You need a quick routine for the answers that matter: numbers, names, dates, rules, and anything you'll send to someone else.

Why confident doesn't mean correct

An AI model writes the most likely next words, based on patterns it learned. It doesn't look things up unless it is searching the web or reading a file you gave it. So a smooth, detailed answer can still contain a made-up detail.

  • Riskiest: specific numbers, dates, prices, names, laws and rules, quotes, and sources.
  • Also risky: anything recent. The AI's knowledge stops at a cut-off date unless it searches the web.
  • Usually safer: rewriting your own text, explaining a general idea, brainstorming.

Five quick checks

  1. 1Ask where each fact came from, then open the source yourself.
  2. 2Recalculate key numbers with a calculator or in Excel.
  3. 3Check names, dates and titles against an official page.
  4. 4For laws, tax and company rules, confirm with the official source or an expert. Rules change, and details depend on your country.
  5. 5Ask the AI to review its own answer (prompt below), then check what it flags.

Numbers need extra care

Weak prompt

What's the total and the average?

Better prompt

Calculate the total and the average of these 12 values. Show each step, then give the final numbers in a small table so I can check them.

Why it works: Asking for the steps makes mistakes visible. You can check each line instead of trusting one number.

Let the spreadsheet do the maths

For anything important, ask the AI for the Excel formula instead of the result. Excel calculates; the AI explains.

Sources that don't exist

AI can produce references that look real but aren't: a report title, an article, a link. Before you quote anything:

  • Open every link. If it doesn't load, or says something else, don't use it.
  • Search for the exact title of any report or article.
  • Prefer official sources: government sites, the company's own page, the original research.

Never pass on a source you haven't opened

If you can't find the source, treat the fact as unverified.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting an answer because it's detailed

    Detail is not proof. Invented facts often come with confident detail.

  • Checking only the easy parts

    Check the parts that would cause harm if wrong: numbers, names, dates and rules.

  • Asking "are you sure?" and stopping there

    The AI may simply agree, or change its answer. Check an outside source.

Try it now: 5 minutes

Try it with a real task from your week. Keep this page open in another tab.

  1. 1Ask a factual question about your field that you already know the answer to.
  2. 2Send the self-check prompt above.
  3. 3Pick one fact it marked as 'sure' and check it in an official source.
  4. 4Notice which kinds of details needed checking, and check those first next time.
